1. The game in one screen

Think multiplayer Snake on a wrap-around ocean floor. Each team controls a swarm of snake-shaped "dragons". Dragons eat pearls to grow, crash into things and die, and can split themselves in two. You write one program; the engine runs a separate copy of it for every dragon you own.

Map10 to 64 tiles per side. Edges wrap around. Always symmetric (mirror or 180° rotation), so both teams get a fair board.
Length of a gameAt most 500 rounds. In each round every living dragon takes exactly one turn.
Your unitsThe map file decides your starting dragons (the 11×11 arena gives 1 each, length 3). Splitting makes more, up to 64 alive at once.
One program, many copiesNo shared memory between dragons. A child from a split starts with a blank memory. Sonar is the only way to talk.
SightEach dragon sees a 7×7 square (49 tiles) around its own head. Nothing else.
The only resourcePearls. Eating one = +1 length. Dead dragons turn half their body into pearls.
Compute100 million CPU points per dragon per turn in the judge's sandbox. Run out and that dragon dies.
How you winWipe out every enemy dragon. Otherwise at round 500: team with the longest single living dragon wins, then total length, then draw.
The scoring rule shapes everything. A pile of small dragons loses to one long dragon at round 500. But small dragons collect pearls faster and see more of the map. Balancing that is the core strategic question.

2. What happens in one round

The engine is a loop. Once you understand its order, most "why did my dragon die?" replays explain themselves.

A round

1Pearls tick. Every tile's countdown drops by 1. At 0 the tile spawns a pearl if nothing is on it, then draws a fresh countdown.
2Dragons act in id order. #0, then #1, then #2... When #5 moves, #0 to #4 have already moved this round and #6 onward have not.
3New children act last. A dragon born from a split this round gets the next id and takes its turn at the end of this same round.
4End check. A team with zero dragons loses. At round 500 the length tiebreaks decide.

One dragon's turn

1Engine → your program. Round number, your length and facing, sonar messages, then the 49 tiles you can see, every dragon segment in view, and the kelp/portal edges.
2Your program thinks. Parse, decide. This is all our code. Budget: 100M points.
3Your program → engine. One action (MOVE or SPLIT), optional SONAR pings and debug drawings, then ENDTURN.
4Engine applies it. The action happens, then sonar rays fly if the dragon survived. No valid action means the dragon dies.

What a reply looks like

LOG heading for the pearl at 4,7
MOVE NNE
SONAR E 90210
PROTOCOL 3
ENDTURN

If you print several actions, only the last one counts. If you print none, crash, or run out of budget, the dragon dies that turn.

What the Python template does

ct, game = unswbc.init()      # read ID, TEAM, MAP
while unswbc.update(ct, game): # read this turn
    execute_turn()             # OUR CODE
    unswbc.end_turn()          # print ENDTURN, flush

The helper library handles the text protocol. Our job is almost entirely execute_turn().

    5. How many dragons will we have?
    
    
    
      
    
        
    At the start
    
        
    Whatever the map says. Each map file lists starting dragons with their positions, usually length 3, and gives ids in file order. The bundled 11×11 arena gives 1 dragon per team. Other maps may give more.
    
        
    During the game
    
        
    Only splitting creates dragons. Cap: 64 alive per team. Every split needs the child to be at least 2 long and the parent to keep at least 2, so a length-L dragon can make at most about L/2 dragons of length 2.
    
        
    What that means for our code
    
        
    64 dragons = 64 separate running copies of our program, each with its own 100M point budget and its own blank memory. Nobody is "in charge". Any coordination has to come from each dragon reasoning about what it sees, or from sonar numbers.
    
      
    
      
    
        
    The split trade-off in real numbers
    
        
    
          
    One dragon, length 8Split 4 + 4
    
          
    Tiles seen per turn49up to 98
    
          
    Pearls eaten per round (max)12
    
          
    Longest dragon84
    
          
    Pearls dropped if killed42 each
    
          
    One head-on crash loseseverythinghalf the team
    
        
    
        
    Splits are irreversible (dragons never merge), and round 500 is scored by the single longest dragon. A plausible plan: split early to harvest, then late in the game funnel growth into one "king" dragon. A dragon that dies next to the king drops half its length as pearls the king can eat. Worth testing.
    
        
    One thing the simulator in section 4 shows clearly: with these simple bots, the splitting team usually wins by elimination long before round 500. Every extra dragon is another wall the enemy can crash into. So the tiebreak only matters if both teams survive.
    
      
    
    
    
  
    

    


    
  
    
    
    6. The compute budget
    
    
    The judge runs our bot in a WebAssembly sandbox and charges fixed "points" per instruction, not wall time. Each dragon gets 100,000,000 points per turn. Figures below are from the docs.
    
    
    
    
    Your local unswbc run does not enforce this; it only has a 10 second wall clock. Always test with unswbc run --sandbox -v, which prints points used per turn. The first turn is charged too, including your imports.
    
    
    In Python, parsing the round already eats about 10% of the budget, and one 32×32 flood fill another 19%. C++ is about 60× cheaper for the same search. Python is fine to start; if our search gets deeper we may port the hot loop to C++.
    
  
    

    


    
  
    
    
    7. What we have to build
    
    
    The whole bot is one function that runs once per dragon per turn. A sensible shape:
    

    def execute_turn():
    moves = legal_moves()              # no kelp, no bodies, not our own tail
    moves = drop_head_on_risks(moves)  # avoid tiles an enemy head can also reach
    if should_split():                 # e.g. long enough, safe spot, under 64
        ct.do_split(ct.get_length() // 2); return
    target = nearest_pearl()           # BFS inside the 7x7 window
    ct.make_move(best(moves, target))  # prefer target, open space, safety
    maybe_send_sonar()                 # share what we saw

    2. Never die by accident.
      Legal-move filter: kelp, bodies, the own-tail rule, the backwards move, illegal splits, unpayable sprints. Always print an action. Most early rating comes from simply not suiciding.
      3. 
      
    3. Hunt pearls.
      BFS to the nearest visible pearl. Use tile countdowns: a tile at countdown 2 is a pearl two rounds from now. Watch out for enemy heads near targets.
      4. 
      
    4. Decide the split policy.
      When to split, how big, and when to stop splitting and grow a king for the round-500 tiebreak. Test with the 10-game comparison habit.
      5. 
      
    5. Use sonar.
      A 64-bit number per direction per turn. Encode things like "pearl cluster at (x, y)" or "enemy head at (x, y)". Echo counts are a free radar. Remember enemies can hear too.
      6. 
      
    6. Exploit the map.
      Symmetry means knowing our own start tells us where the enemy started. Portals are shortcuts; kelp corridors are traps to push enemies into.
      7. 
      
    7. Fight.
      Cut off enemy dragons with our bodies, force them into walls, and take head-on trades only when our dragon is the shorter one.
      8. 
      
    8. Stay in budget.ongoing
      Check points with --sandbox -v. Leave margin. Consider C++ if Python gets tight.
